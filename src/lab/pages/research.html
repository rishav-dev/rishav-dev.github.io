Extended validation · methodology

Does the simulator generalize, and can search beat a hand-tuned design?

The bench (P1–P6) measures reliability on the pills the concepts were designed for. This page asks three narrower, more falsifiable questions of the same simulator: which inputs actually drive the outcome, does the geometry hold up on pills nobody looked at while building the mechanisms, and does treating a mechanism's dimensions as a real optimization problem, searched, then checked on data the search never saw, beat the analytic baseline. The last section is the honest boundary: what none of this shows without a physical machine.

0 · Is the adjustable-cone idea novel, and what else is worth trying?

A quick, honest prior-art check on the cone-and-gate mechanism built below (§4), plus two alternative principles that came out of reading that prior art. This is a limited keyword search of public patent databases, not a patentability opinion, not a freedom-to-operate search, and not legal advice. Only a patent attorney or agent doing a proper search can tell you whether something is actually patentable.

What the search found

Every individual element of the cone-and-gate idea already has close prior art:

Reading it. Individually, none of these pieces look new. A patent claim generally has to be novel and non-obvious over the combination of everything already public (35 U.S.C. § 103), and combining an adjustable funnel, an auto-sizing sensor and a sensor-gated valve the way this idea does is arguably the obvious way to combine three well-known parts, each already combined with the others somewhere in the list above. The part that is not in any of these results is the specific construction, a cone built from a bundle of individually-adjustable wires rather than a solid iris, sleeve or panel, which is a narrower, more defensible place to look for novelty than the overall idea. That is exactly the kind of question a patent search only a professional can really answer.

Since this table was first written, the simulation added an anti-arch drive to the same mechanism: the two sides of the feed cone are driven in antiphase (about ±1 mm at 50 Hz, roughly 10 g, an ordinary electromagnetic feeder drive), so the gap a two-pill arch has to span pulses open and shut, which a shared side-to-side shake can never do. As the new row above shows, vibrating a hopper's own walls to break exactly this kind of arch is not new, it is a mature idea in bulk-materials handling going back over fifty years. That reinforces the overall verdict rather than changing it; nothing found in this pass moves the narrow construction-level angle identified above.

Alternative A · Compliant-membrane pinch array

Replace the rigid wire cone with a single silicone tube whose wall carries a ring of small independently-driven pinch points (like a Braille cell), so the effective bore is set by which pinchers are engaged rather than by moving a rigid part. Search for prior art specifically combining soft-robotic / pneumatic membrane actuation with pill singulation came back thin, mostly general soft-robotics literature and one dispenser patent using elastomeric rollers, not a membrane array, which is a weaker signal of "already claimed" than everything in the table above, not a clearance.

Trade-off: continuous, gentle, no rigid impact surface (the cue-ball ejection problem found in §4 below likely disappears), but pneumatics or many small actuators are a real manufacturing and reliability cost, and food/pharma-safe elastomer membranes need their own qualification.

Alternative B · Differential-friction walking floor

A short section of floor made from narrow strips, alternating strips driven at two different speeds (like the belt concept E, but as a 2-D array rather than two single belts), so a pill's orientation determines which strips grip it and which slip, long pills get walked end-first, round pills roll to a stop against a fixed rail. No dedicated funnel geometry to size for each pill family.

This is closer to established parts-feeder engineering (vibratory and belt singulation are both well precedented, including in the results above), so the honest expectation is that it inherits belt-style patent crowding rather than opening new ground, it is proposed here as a robustness alternative to the cone (it doesn't depend on forcing an orientation through a fixed pipe diameter), not as a stronger patent candidate.

Concept H · vacuum-lip airlock, the same honest check

H came out of a request for a design nobody has used before. That cannot be promised by anyone without a professional search, and the quick keyword search behind this card found the same pattern as for G: every principle H uses is already public.

Element of HClose prior art found
Holding one pill on a suction port to singulate itUS 2008/0061074 "Vacuum based pill singulator and counter"; WO 2003/051721 "Vacuum drum pill counter"; US 6,053,302, pharmacy counting with vacuum pick-up tubes; concepts B and F on this site
A feed belt under a counter-rotating singulating rollerStandard bulk-parts feeding practice; concept E on this site uses the identical belt and roller
Suction holding items at a conveyor, released at a set pointUS 7,387,298 / US 7,866,662 "Synchronized vacuum belt feeder"; vacuum belt conveyors in general
Two stops that are never open together (an escapement or "airlock")A classical mechanism, the two-pin escapement is how concept G's pin and gate work, and rocker escapements appear throughout parts feeding

What the search did not turn up is the specific arrangement: two fixed suction ports at a conveyor's lip acting as the two stops of that escapement, with the held lead pill itself physically blocking the next one, and the lip re-arming the instant the released pill has cleared it. "Not found in a handful of searches" is a long way from "new". An examiner could well call it an obvious combination of a vacuum hold with a two-stop escapement; if anything here is claimable, it is that narrow construction, not the idea of holding pills with suction.

How H got here. Two earlier candidates were built in this simulator and dropped because the physics said no:

  • A swinging spout that pours. The lower funnel and a ledge form one part that tilts to pour pills over a lip. The pile under the funnel sticks until the tilt is well past the point where a single pill would slide, then lets go all at once, so pills reached the lip too fast to stop. A pivot far above the ledge also flung pills during the swing-back, and a tilt large enough to feed sticky pills opened a gap at the joint with the fixed neck.
  • A single port on the lip. The belt backs off after the port catches a pill. A rolling pill only takes up about a third of any change in belt speed, so backing off doesn't pull round pills back, and the pile keeps pushing the queue forward while the belt is stopped. The second port, holding the next pill directly, is what fixed that.

Model notes specific to H. Pick-up uses the same port-seal test as concept F, but a pill that is already sealed is held by a seat model F never needed. It resists sliding up to 0.6 × the suction force, which is seal-lip friction, and rolling up to the suction force × the port radius, because a sealed pill has to pivot over the rim against the pressure. Suction is capped at 10 × the pill's weight, as everywhere on this site. Before a seal forms, the pull through the gap is limited to 1 × the pill's weight within 0.8 mm; F's model allows up to 10 × from 3 mm. These are modelled values, not measurements of a real port. H was also tuned against the P1 preset pills, so read its P1 score next to the held-out challenge result in §2, which it never saw.

1 · What actually drives the outcome?

Variance-based sensitivity (η², one-way ANOVA effect size) computed directly from the bench data already on this site (P3–P6), no new simulation. For each concept and factor, η² is the share of the variance in dose-exact outcomes explained by that factor's level; 0 % means the factor made no difference in this sweep, 100 % means it explained all of it. This tells you which knob is worth turning first, and, just as usefully, which one is not the problem.

2 · Held-out generalization: pills nobody tuned against

Every concept's geometry (funnel angle, pocket/slot auto-sizing, lane height, singulator gap, cup diameter…) is an analytic function of the pill's own length and width, never a lookup table, never fit to an outcome. The five pills below sit outside both the eight product presets and the 25-size P2 sweep: an oversize tablet, a micro tablet, a nearly-round caplet, an extreme-aspect-ratio capsule, and a nearly-round softgel. None of them existed while the mechanisms were being built or debugged. This is a within-simulation generalization test, it says the formulas hold up on unseen geometry, not that the physics matches a real machine (§4).

3 · Search vs. the hand-tuned baseline: an honest train/test experiment

The shuttle's pocket sizing is an analytic formula (§2), not a fit, so does treating it as an actual optimization problem do better? A random search over pocket length × depth was scored on 8 search seeds; the incumbent and the analytic baseline were then both re-scored on 16 held-out seeds the search never touched. Reporting the held-out number, not the search number, is what keeps this from being a demo of overfitting.

4 · ProDoseBench: a reproducible protocol

The six protocols behind this whole site (P1–P6, plus the sensing Monte-Carlo and the chute sweeps) are a fixed, versioned, seeded specification, not one-off numbers. Anyone with the repository can regenerate every chart on this site byte-for-byte from the same seeds, or point the same trial code (js/trial.js) at a different mechanism.

What's fixed

Pill geometry model, bottle geometry, the physics engine and contact solver, the trial harness (js/trial.js, shared by Node and the browser), the metric definitions (single-pill, dose-exact, jam, damage) and the random seeds for every protocol.

What's swept

P1 reliability (8 pills × 12 fills), P2 size range (25 sizes), P3 adjustment (per-concept setting grid), P4 last-pills / fill level, P5 motor limit (tuned machine), P6 stress test (tight clearance, rigid lips).

Reproduce it

node bench/run.js        # P1–P6 → data/bench.js
node bench/sensing.js    # confirmation Monte-Carlo
node bench/chute.js      # transport & collection
node bench/challenge.js  # held-out geometry (§2)
node bench/optimize.js   # search case study (§3)

Extend it

A new concept implements the same design interface (bounds, control, sense, compat…) and registers in P.DESIGNS, the bench, sensing lab and this page pick it up with no other changes.

5 · What this cannot show

Being specific about the boundary is part of the method, not a disclaimer at the end of it.

Sim-to-real transfer

Nothing on this site has been checked against a physical machine. Friction, restitution, crush strength and drop ratings are typical handbook values for each pill class, not measurements of specific tablets, and the contact model takes the larger static friction of the two materials in contact (Matter.js's convention), which is a conservative bias for low-friction linings. A calibration/validation split against real hardware (build on a subset of pills, predict on a held-out subset, measure the gap) is the next real test, and it needs physical pills, a physical rig, and camera or sensor ground truth, none of which exist in this session.

Physical fault injection

The simulator can stall a motor or lose a vacuum seal because a parameter is set that way. It cannot tell you what a real stepper does when a real gear tooth actually skips, or what a real belt does after a thousand hours of wear. That requires a built mechanism.

Sensor ground truth

The sensing lab's six confirmation methods are evaluated against the simulator's own pill positions, a fair test of the method, but the noise model (12 mg load-cell noise, idealized photodiode shadows) is assumed, not measured from a real beam or scale.
